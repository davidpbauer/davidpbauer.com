The Work of Water and Stone

Best read slowly, in silence.
← Read “The Water Beneath”

This reflection sits where vocation meets livelihood, where the need to sustain a family meets the ache to serve something larger than survival. I work in academic technology, but the questions underneath my days are older than any system I support: what endures when the structures shift, and how do I remain human in the work that feeds us?

I live between what feeds and what fulfills.
Between the need to provide and the need to serve something larger than provision.

My tools are circuits, networks, and code — but what I am trying to build is belonging, clarity, and the quiet dignity of learning.

In the language of systems, I am supposed to deliver outcomes.
But the real work happens where no metric reaches: when a student finds courage through design that makes space for them, when an instructor rediscovers wonder through technology that listens instead of shouts.

That is the work of water — soft, unseen, but shaping stone with persistence.

The stone is the structure I inhabit: budgets, dashboards, deadlines.
It is heavy, necessary, and indifferent to longing.

I do not despise it; it anchors me, sustains my family, gives me the ground to stand on.
But every day I ask how to let the water move through it — how to carve channels where meaning can flow without shattering what must still hold weight.

This, I think, is the craft of faithfulness in an age of collapse:
to remain porous when the world hardens,
to let vocation seep through the fractures of necessity,
to trust that erosion is not failure but the patient art of transformation.

I am not trying to save the institution.
I am trying to keep the water moving.

Because when the stone finally breaks — as all empires do —
it is the water that will remember the shape of the valley.

A reflection on vocation, livelihood, and the quiet work of staying human in emerging technology.

My path into higher education was not a straight line, but a discovery of purpose through technology and strategy. I lead academic technology because it lets my technical instincts serve human learning rather than replace it. If you also live in this tension — responsible for systems, but haunted by questions of meaning — I hope these words give you language for the work you already know in your heart.
